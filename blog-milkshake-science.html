The physics
of a proper malt

By Aaron Vasquez, owner February 2, 2026 8 minute read

In this article

  • Why yours turns to soup
  • Overrun, and why it matters
  • What a spindle actually does
  • Butterfat and mouthfeel
  • What malt powder is
  • The temperature window
  • How we build one

The Albuquerque counter has six Hamilton Beach spindle mixers. Two of them are original to the 1954 build and have been rewound but never replaced. People ask about them constantly, usually while holding a shake and trying to work out why it is not the same as the one they made at home. Here is the answer, at more length than anyone asked for.

Why yours turns to soup

The single most common home mistake is using a countertop blender. A blender is designed to reduce solids to liquid as fast as possible, using a small, fast, aggressively angled blade in a narrow well. Applied to ice cream it does exactly that: it shears the structure apart, warms the mix through friction, and gives you a cold sweet drink with no body.

A shake is not supposed to be a liquid. It is supposed to be a partially destabilised frozen foam that has been softened just enough to move through a straw and no further. Those are two genuinely different physical states, and the equipment you use decides which one you get.

The second common mistake is the ratio. Most home recipes call for something between a third and a half cup of milk per two scoops. That is roughly double what we use. Milk is the thing that turns a foam into a drink, and it should be added by the tablespoon while the machine is already running, not poured in at the start.

Overrun, and why it matters

Overrun is the industry word for how much air has been whipped into a frozen dessert. If you start with one litre of liquid mix and end with two litres of ice cream, you have 100 percent overrun, and half of what you are eating is air.

Cheap supermarket ice cream runs at 90 to 100 percent overrun because air is free and it sells by volume. Premium ice cream sits between 20 and 50 percent. Frozen custard, which is what we use, is legally required in the United States to contain at least 1.4 percent egg yolk solids, and is typically churned to somewhere between 15 and 35 percent overrun.

This matters for a shake because air is the first thing you lose in a spindle mixer. High overrun ice cream collapses almost immediately, the volume drops, and you are left with a thin puddle plus whatever milk you added. Low overrun custard has less air to lose, so it keeps its density right through mixing. Our base runs at about 22 percent overrun, which is on the dense end even for custard, and it is the main reason a Starlite shake does not deflate on the walk to a booth.

What a spindle actually does

A spindle mixer is a single shaft with a small agitator disc on the end, spinning at somewhere between 10,000 and 18,000 rpm in a tall narrow cup. The disc is not a blade. It has no cutting edge at all. It works by dragging the mixture into a vortex and folding it back on itself, which does two things a blender cannot.

First, it aerates rather than shears. The vortex pulls air down from the surface and distributes it as fine bubbles through the mix, adding structure instead of destroying it. Second, because the cup is tall and narrow and the disc sits low, the mix circulates vertically rather than being pinned against the walls. Product at the top of the cup stays colder for longer, so the whole thing warms more slowly.

The operator's job is mostly about depth. You start with the disc near the bottom, raise it slowly as the mix loosens, and drop it back down for the final few seconds to pull the top layer through. A cook who leaves the spindle in one position for forty seconds gets a shake that is liquid at the bottom and solid at the top. It takes about a week of practice to stop doing that.

Butterfat and mouthfeel

Our custard base is 14 percent butterfat, which comes from a family creamery outside Madison, Wisconsin. Legally, ice cream in the United States needs 10 percent. Most commercial product sits at 10 or 11 because butterfat is the expensive part.

Fat does two jobs here. It coats the tongue, which is what people are describing when they call a shake rich rather than sweet. More usefully, fat globules physically get in the way of ice crystals forming large, so a higher fat mix stays smooth for longer as it warms. A 10 percent base and a 14 percent base taste similar at the moment they leave the spindle. Ten minutes later, sitting in a booth, they are not close.

There is a ceiling. Above about 16 percent the mix starts to feel waxy and the flavour of whatever you added gets muffled by the fat. We tried an 18 percent base in 2017 and quietly went back.

What malt powder is

Malted milk powder is three things ground together: barley that has been sprouted and dried, wheat flour, and evaporated milk solids. It was invented in the 1870s as an infant food and turned out to taste extremely good in a milkshake, which is more or less the whole history.

The sprouted barley is doing the interesting work. Germination converts some of the grain's starch into maltose and produces a set of nutty, slightly savoury aromatic compounds that survive drying. In a shake that reads as depth. It is the difference between a chocolate shake, which tastes like chocolate and milk, and a chocolate malt, which tastes like chocolate, milk and something toasted underneath.

We use two level scoops, about 18 grams, per 16 ounce shake. It also thickens slightly, because of the wheat flour, which is a small bonus. If you are buying it for home use, look for one where barley malt is the first ingredient rather than sugar.

The temperature window

Custard comes out of the freezer at 0 degrees Fahrenheit and is tempered in a holding cabinet at 8 to 10 degrees before service. Straight from a deep freeze it is too hard to mix without either burning out the motor or over-running the machine to compensate.

The shake leaves the spindle at about 21 degrees Fahrenheit. Below 18 it will not move through a straw and people give up on it. Above 25 it starts visibly separating within five minutes. That seven degree window is the entire game, and it is why we serve into a glass that has been sitting in the freezer and why the leftover comes to the table still in the metal tin, which holds the cold far better than glass does.

How we build one

Frozen glass out of the freezer. Two number twelve scoops of custard into a chilled steel tin. Syrup and malt powder on top of the custard, never underneath, or it welds to the bottom of the tin. Three tablespoons of whole milk. Spindle in, forty seconds, moving the disc up and down through the mix. Pour into the frozen glass, which will take about two thirds of it. Whipped cream, cherry, and the tin comes to the table alongside with the rest.

That last part is not generosity, incidentally. A 16 ounce shake does not fit in a 12 ounce glass, and every fountain in America solved that problem the same way in about 1935. It just happens to feel like a gift, which is why nobody has changed it since.

The malted chocolate shake is $8.25 and on the fountain menu in all three rooms. The counter stools face the spindles if you want to watch one being made.
