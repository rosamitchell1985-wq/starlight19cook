What the interstate
took from 66

By Rosalie Vasquez, owner 1986 to 2016 January 18, 2026 10 minute read

In this article

  • The drive
  • What actually happened
  • Tucumcari
  • Who survived, and why
  • Against nostalgia
  • What we took from it

My father lit the griddle at 2001 Central Avenue in September 1954, which meant the Starlite got thirty one good years before the interstate arrived and roughly six bad ones afterwards. I ran the place through most of the bad ones. Last October I drove the old alignment from Tucumcari to Kingman with my grandson, partly for the company and partly because I wanted to see how much of it was still standing.

The drive

It is about 730 miles if you stay off Interstate 40 wherever the frontage road allows, which is more of the way than you would expect. New Mexico and Arizona both kept long stretches of the original roadbed as state or business routes, so you can drive genuine 1930s alignment for an hour at a time, at 45 miles an hour, past buildings that have been empty since the Reagan administration.

We counted. Between Tucumcari and Kingman we passed 61 structures that had unambiguously been roadside food businesses: diners, cafes, drive-ins, and the kind of place that sold gas at the front and hamburgers at the back. Eleven were operating. Nine had been converted to something else, mostly antique shops and one very good taqueria. Forty one were derelict, and of those, fourteen still had a legible sign.

That ratio, roughly one in six still doing the thing it was built to do, matches what the preservation surveys have found since the 1990s. It is not a story about a few unlucky businesses. It is a story about an entire economic layer being removed in about fifteen years.

What actually happened

The Interstate Highway System was authorised in 1956 and built in sections over the next three decades. Interstate 40 replaced Route 66 across most of its western length. The last section of Route 66 to be bypassed was at Williams, Arizona, in October 1984, and Route 66 was formally decertified as a US highway in 1985.

The mechanism was simple and it was not malicious. An old highway runs through the middle of a town, so everyone driving past has to slow to 25 miles an hour, look at your sign, and decide whether they are hungry. An interstate runs around the town at 70 miles an hour with a numbered exit and a services sign that lists whoever paid to be listed. The traffic did not disappear. It stopped being reachable.

The businesses that had built their entire model on interception lost between half and all of their trade, usually within two years of the bypass opening. In Albuquerque our own drop was about two thirds inside eighteen months, and we were in a city of 350,000 people. In a town of 2,000 there was frequently nothing left to fall back on at all.

Tucumcari

Tucumcari is the clearest case I know. At its peak it advertised itself on billboards for hundreds of miles in both directions with the line "Tucumcari Tonite" and a count of available motel rooms, which at one point was over 2,000 in a town whose population has never exceeded about 8,000.

Interstate 40 bypassed it in 1981. The strip along Tucumcari Boulevard, which is the old 66 alignment, still has the buildings, and a handful of them, notably the Blue Swallow Motel and a couple of restaurants, are open and doing well precisely because people now drive there on purpose. But the density is gone. What was a working commercial street with maybe forty food and lodging businesses is now perhaps eight, spread over three miles, with long gaps of empty lots and shells.

My grandson, who is 24, thought it looked romantic. I understand why. When you did not watch it happen, a faded sign is a photograph. When you did, it is somebody's father who lost a building he had put twenty five years into, and who by 1986 was driving a delivery truck in Amarillo.

Who survived, and why

I have spent a long time thinking about this, mostly because in 1986 I needed to know the answer urgently. From what I have seen, the survivors fell into three groups.

The first group were in places big enough to have a local population that could support them without highway traffic. That was us. Albuquerque had a university, three hospitals, a rail yard and a music venue within a mile of our door. We did not survive because we were good. We survived because there were enough people already there, and because we stopped pretending the highway was coming back.

The second group were close enough to an interstate exit to recapture some of the traffic, and were willing to rebuild their sign, their parking and often their whole frontage to face the new road. That cost money most independent operators did not have, which is a large part of why the chains did so well out of the transition. A franchise had access to capital and a national reservations network. A family diner had a mortgage and a good reputation within ten miles.

The third group, and the smallest, survived on destination value. They were sufficiently distinctive that people would leave the interstate deliberately to find them. That is a real strategy and it works, but it takes decades to build and it is fragile. Several of the places now presented as Route 66 icons spent the 1990s within months of closing.

Against nostalgia

There is a version of this history that treats the interstate as a villain and the old road as a lost paradise. I do not have much patience for it.

Route 66 was dangerous. Two lanes, no median, heavy truck traffic, and the stretch through the Black Mountains in Arizona killed people regularly enough that plenty of drivers paid to have their cars towed over it. The interstate is safer by an enormous margin, it moves freight far more efficiently, and the towns that lost their through traffic also lost a great deal of noise, diesel and risk to their children.

What I object to is not the road. It is the idea that the cost was nobody's fault and therefore nobody's problem. The bypasses were planned years ahead. In most cases the businesses on the old alignment were given no meaningful transition support and, in a fair number of towns, were not consulted about exit placement at all. Two miles either way on an off ramp decided whether a family kept its livelihood, and those decisions were made in offices a long way from Tucumcari.

What we took from it

Two things, and they still govern how we run the company.

The first is that a restaurant which depends on passing traffic is not a restaurant, it is a toll booth with a kitchen. The moment the traffic moves, you are finished. Everything we do now is aimed at being useful to the people who live within a mile of each door: opening early, opening late, keeping breakfast on all day for people working odd shifts, and holding prices where a hospital worker can eat with us twice a week rather than twice a year.

The second is that you do not cut hours in a downturn. When we were down two thirds, every piece of advice I got was to close Mondays and stop serving after nine. I did not, and I think it was the only genuinely good decision I made that decade. A neighbourhood reads reduced hours as the beginning of a departure, and once people stop assuming you will be open, they stop checking.

We are the only building on our block that has been in continuous operation since 1954. That is not a boast about quality. It is mostly luck of geography, plus one stubborn refusal to turn the sign off.
