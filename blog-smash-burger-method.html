How we smash
a burger

By Delfina Arreola, executive chef March 14, 2026 9 minute read

In this article

  • The grind
  • Portioning and temperature
  • Griddle heat
  • The press itself
  • When to salt
  • Cheese, onion, bun
  • Doing this at home

We cook somewhere around 1,900 smash burgers a week across three rooms. The method below is what we teach a new cook in their first shift, and it takes most people about two weeks to get consistently right. None of it is secret and none of it is complicated. It is just specific.

The grind

Seventy percent chuck to thirty percent short rib, ground once through a 4.5mm plate. That works out to roughly 22 percent fat, which is higher than most people expect and considerably higher than the 80/20 you buy at a supermarket.

The ratio matters less than the two things underneath it. First, the fat has to be intramuscular rather than added trim. Chuck and short rib both carry their fat through the muscle rather than in a cap, so when the patty hits the griddle the fat renders evenly across the whole surface instead of pooling at one edge. Second, we grind once, not twice. A double grind gives you a smoother, more uniform texture that behaves like a sausage. You want the meat slightly ragged, because the ragged edges are what catch on the steel and turn into the crust.

We grind at 7am for that day only. Beef ground the night before loses about a quarter of its ability to hold its shape under pressure, and you can taste the difference by the second day. This is not fussiness, it is the single biggest variable in the whole process. If you change nothing else, grind closer to service.

Portioning and temperature

Four ounces per patty, loosely balled, never pressed into a puck. A cook rolls a tray of 40 balls at a time and they go straight back into the walk-in on a sheet tray, uncovered, for at least twenty minutes.

The balls go on the griddle cold, between 36 and 40 degrees Fahrenheit. This is the opposite of the advice you will read for a thick grilled burger, where you rest the meat to room temperature so the middle cooks through. A smash burger is a quarter inch thick. There is no middle to worry about. What you actually want is the greatest possible temperature difference between the surface of the meat and the surface of the steel, because that difference is what drives the browning reaction hard and fast before the interior has time to overcook.

A room temperature ball, smashed on the same griddle, gives you a grey, evenly cooked patty with a thin patchy crust. A cold one gives you a dark lacy edge and a centre that is still juicy. Same meat, same griddle, entirely different burger.

Griddle heat

Our flat tops run at 450 degrees Fahrenheit at the burger station, measured with an infrared thermometer at the start of every shift and again after the lunch push. That is hotter than a home cast iron pan will comfortably hold and slightly cooler than the 500 degrees some kitchens use.

We tried 500 for about six weeks in 2018. It builds crust faster, but it also burns the shaved onion before the patty is done and it drives so much moisture out of the meat that the burger goes dry inside two minutes on the pass. At 450 the crust takes about fifteen seconds longer and the burger survives the walk to a booth. That fifteen seconds is the trade we chose.

Steel temperature drops the moment cold meat touches it. On a full griddle we cook eight patties at a time in two rows of four, never more, because a ninth patty pulls the surface temperature below 400 and the whole batch steams instead of searing. In a home pan the equivalent number is one, possibly two. This is the mistake almost everyone makes.

The press itself

A stiff, wide metal press, not a spatula. We use a six inch flat press with a square of parchment between the press and the meat, because meat sticks to steel and the parchment prevents you tearing the top of the patty off when you lift.

The press goes down hard and once, within five seconds of the ball touching the griddle. Weight goes straight down, held for eight to ten seconds, then lifted cleanly. Not a wiggle, not a second press, not a top up halfway through the cook. Every additional press after that first one squeezes out rendered fat that would otherwise stay in the patty, and you can watch the burger get worse in real time.

The five second window is the part that takes practice. Smash immediately and the outside of the ball has not started to set, so the meat spreads unevenly and tears. Wait fifteen seconds and the bottom surface has already contracted, so the patty resists spreading and comes out thick in the middle and thin at the edge. Five seconds is when the ball is just tacky enough to hold together and still soft enough to flatten to an even quarter inch.

Total cook time is about ninety seconds on the first side, then a flip, then thirty seconds with the cheese on. That is it. If the burger is on the griddle for three minutes, something went wrong.

When to salt

After the smash, never before. This is the point people argue with us about most often, so here is the reasoning.

Salt draws moisture to the surface of meat and begins dissolving muscle proteins, which is exactly what you want for a sausage and exactly what you do not want here. Salted ground beef becomes tacky and springy within minutes, and a tacky ball does not spread properly under a press. It also holds its moisture at the surface, which then has to boil off before browning can start, costing you crust.

So the ball goes down unsalted, gets smashed flat, and is salted from about ten inches up, in one even pass, immediately after the press comes off. Kosher salt, about half a gram per patty. Black pepper goes on at the same time. The salt hits a hot dry surface, dissolves into the rendering fat rather than into the meat, and seasons the crust itself.

Cheese, onion, bun

American cheese, and I will defend that in a separate piece. It is an emulsified cheese product engineered to melt without breaking, which is precisely the job in front of it. Cheddar on a smash burger splits into oil and curd every time. The cheese goes on straight after the flip so it has thirty seconds of contact heat.

Shaved white onion goes onto the raw ball before the smash on our green chile burger and on request elsewhere. It gets pressed into the meat, half steams and half fries, and comes out somewhere between an ingredient and a seasoning. The onion has to be shaved on a mandoline to about one millimetre. Any thicker and it stays raw in the middle.

The bun is a potato roll, split and griddled in beef fat on the same surface, about forty seconds, until the cut face is dark gold. An untoasted bun turns to paste against a juicy patty. We toast every single one, including on takeout orders where it would be easier not to.

Doing this at home

You can get very close with a cast iron pan and no special equipment. Buy chuck and ask the butcher to grind it once, coarse. Roll four ounce balls and chill them. Get the pan hotter than feels sensible, cook one patty at a time, smash it with the back of a stiff metal spatula weighted with a can, hold for ten seconds, salt after. Toast the bun in the fat left behind.

The two things you cannot easily replicate are the surface area of a commercial flat top and the fact that our griddle has had beef fat cooked into it continuously since 1954. The first one you can work around by cooking fewer patties. The second one takes about seventy years.

The burger described here is The Starlite Double, $14.50, on the menu in all three rooms. If you want to watch it happen, the counter seats face the griddle and are always walk-in.
