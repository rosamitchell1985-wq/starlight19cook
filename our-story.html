Seventy years
on one street

A Quonset hut, a second hand griddle and a neon sign bought on credit. Everything since has been an argument about whether to change anything.

1954 first griddle lit 1957 Dot's meatloaf 1961 the onion loaf 1985 the interstate bypass 2011 Chicago 2019 Santa Monica

Merle and Dot, and a building nobody wanted

In the spring of 1954 Merle Vasquez came home from three years in Korea with a mechanic's certificate and no interest in using it. He had $1,100 in savings, a wife named Dot who could bake, and an idea that the stretch of Route 66 running through Albuquerque was about to get a great deal busier than it already was.

The building was a surplus Quonset hut that had been sitting behind a tyre shop on Central Avenue since the end of the war. It was half rusted, it had no plumbing, and the landlord let it go for $40 a month because he had given up on finding anyone else. Merle spent four months welding, Dot spent four months testing pie crusts on the neighbours, and on 12 September 1954 they lit a second hand Wolf griddle and opened the door.

The neon sign came later that autumn, bought on a payment plan from a shop in Barelas that also made signs for motels. It cost more than the griddle. Dot thought it was madness. Merle's argument, which he repeated for the next four decades, was that a diner nobody can find at night is not a diner, it is a kitchen with chairs.

What the first menu actually said

We still have it, laminated, hanging by the pass in Albuquerque. Six items. A hamburger at 35 cents. A cheeseburger at 45. Chili, a cup or a bowl. Coffee, bottomless, a nickel. Pie by the slice, whatever Dot had baked. And, written in ballpoint at the bottom because it was added on the second day, a chocolate malt for 20 cents.

Three of those six are still on the menu in a recognisable form. The chili recipe has changed exactly once, in 1979, when Merle switched from kidney to pinto beans after a supplier went under. Regulars complained for a year. Nobody has complained since.

The bad decade

In October 1985 the final stretch of Interstate 40 opened around Albuquerque and the traffic that had fed Central Avenue for thirty years simply stopped. Diners along Route 66 closed at a rate of about one a month across four states. The Starlite lost roughly two thirds of its business inside eighteen months.

What saved it was not clever. Merle and Dot's daughter, Rosalie, took over the books in 1986 and made two calls. The first was to stay open the same hours regardless of how empty the room was, because the moment you cut hours you tell a neighbourhood you are leaving. The second was to stop chasing highway traffic entirely and start feeding the people who actually lived within a mile: hospital shifts, the university, the rail yard, and every band that came through the Sunshine Theater on a Friday night.

It took six years to get back to where they had been. The late night hours we still keep on Fridays and Saturdays are a direct leftover from that decision.

My father used to say a diner is a public utility that happens to sell pie. You do not get to close because it is quiet.

Rosalie Vasquez, owner from 1986 to 2016

Three rooms, and the argument about growth

Rosalie turned down four franchise offers between 1994 and 2008. The Chicago room only happened in 2011 because her son Aaron had been cooking in Wicker Park for a decade and found a building with the right bones, a long narrow footprint that could take a counter down one side. Santa Monica followed in 2019 for a less romantic reason: a landlord who wanted a tenant with a seventy year operating history and was willing to price accordingly.

We have said no to every location since, including two that would have made more money than either of the ones we opened. The limit is simple and it is not a marketing position. Pie is baked in Albuquerque and driven out on Tuesdays and Fridays. Anywhere we cannot reach with a van in a day is a place we cannot serve our own pie, and at that point it would be a different restaurant wearing our sign.

Chef

Executive chef since 2016

Delfina Arreola

Delfina grew up in Belen, forty minutes south, in a family that ran a tortilleria for three generations. She spent eleven years in fine dining, four of them as a sous chef in Santa Fe, and by her own account was thoroughly sick of tweezers by the end of it.

She came to the Starlite in 2014 as a line cook, on purpose and at a pay cut, because she wanted to work somewhere the food was allowed to be finished. Two years later Rosalie handed her the whole kitchen.

Her changes have been careful. The griddle burger got a new grind ratio, 70 percent chuck to 30 percent short rib, coarser than before. The fries went from frozen back to hand cut, which cost more in labour than it saved in product. Green chile is now roasted in house every September, about 900 pounds of it, in a rented drum on the back lot. And the Midnight Griddle exists entirely because she started cooking staff meal properly and people asked to buy a ticket.

What she has refused to change is longer. The meatloaf, the chili, the pie crust, the malt method, the coffee, the fact that breakfast runs all day, and the price of an egg cream.

Who we buy from

Not a sustainability pledge, just a list. These are the people whose names are on the boxes in our walk-in.

Beef

Corriente and Angus chuck and short rib from a co-operative of five ranches in Socorro and Catron counties. Ground in house every morning, never the day before.

Eggs and dairy

Vida Verde Farm in Los Lunas for eggs. Custard base for the fountain comes from a family creamery outside Madison, Wisconsin, at 14 percent butterfat.

Chile

Green from a family in Hatch we have bought from since 1998, roasted by us in September. Red from Chimayo, bought as dried pods and ground here.

Bread

Potato rolls and marble rye from Golden Crown Panaderia in Albuquerque. Chicago and Santa Monica each use a local bakery working from the same spec sheet.

Potatoes

Kennebecs from the San Luis Valley in Colorado, delivered twice a week, cut at 7am and never held more than 36 hours.

Coffee

Roasted for us on Second Street in Albuquerque to a dark profile we have not changed since 2009. Sold by the pound at every register for $16.
